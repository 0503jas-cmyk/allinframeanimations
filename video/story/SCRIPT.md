## 0. HOOK (0:00-1:00)

In August 1914, twenty-eight men sailed south to cross an entire continent. They never even set foot on it.

Instead, they lost their ship to the ice. They lost their way home. They drifted across a frozen sea for months, then rowed three small boats to a rock that no ship ever visits.

And then their leader did something that sounds like a bad joke. He took five men, a lifeboat the size of a garden shed, and sailed eight hundred miles across the most violent ocean on Earth.

All of this happened. And when it was over, every single man from the ship was alive.

This is the story of Ernest Shackleton and the Endurance. It is a story about ice, and hunger, and a navigation that should have been impossible. But mostly, it is a story about what keeps people alive when everything else is gone.

## 1. THE PLAN (1:00-2:30)

By 1914, the map of the world was almost complete. The poles had been reached. Roald Amundsen got to the South Pole in 1911, and Robert Scott died trying to follow him.

Shackleton, an Anglo-Irish explorer who had already come within a hundred miles of the pole, wanted the last great prize. Not the pole itself, but the crossing: walk from one side of Antarctica to the other, passing through the pole on the way. About eighteen hundred miles.

He raised the money, bought a strong wooden ship, and named her Endurance, after his family motto: "By endurance we conquer."

The plan had two halves. Endurance would carry Shackleton and his crossing party into the Weddell Sea. A second ship would come from the other side of the continent, the Ross Sea, laying supply depots for the final stretch.

Twenty-eight men signed up. Among them was Frank Worsley, the captain, a New Zealander who could navigate by almost nothing. Frank Wild, the steady second in command. Tom Crean, a veteran of earlier polar expeditions. And Frank Hurley, a photographer who would carry heavy glass plates through all of it.

On the fifth of December, 1914, Endurance left South Georgia, the last outpost of humans, and sailed into the unknown.

## 2. TRAPPED (2:30-5:00)

Almost at once, things went wrong. The whalers at South Georgia warned that the ice was unusually far north that year. Within two days, the ship met pack ice.

For weeks, Endurance pushed through it, sometimes making only a few miles a day. Then, in January 1915, less than a day's sailing from the coast where they meant to land, the ice closed around the ship, and it did not open again.

They tried everything. The crew went over the side with picks and saws, hacking at the ice. They ran the engines, rocked the ship, tried to break a channel. They gained a few yards. The ice refilled them in hours.

Shackleton understood the situation before anyone else. They were not stuck. They were part of the pack now. The whole mass of ice, thousands of square miles of it, was moving in a slow clockwise circle, and the ship moved with it.

So he made a decision that shaped the rest of the story. He accepted that they would winter in the ice, and he started to protect the men's minds before anything else.

Routine kept everybody busy. Dogs had to be exercised and trained. Scientists measured the ocean. There were football matches on the ice, sled races between the dog teams, and a banjo in the evenings. Shackleton kept the crew mixed, officers working alongside sailors, so that resentment had no place to grow.

By the spring, the ice began to groan. The ship was drifting north, then west, carried by the sea itself, farther and farther from the coast they had come for.

## 3. THE SHIP IS CRUSHED (5:00-7:00)

In summer, the ice should have opened. It didn't. In the autumn of 1915 the pack began to squeeze.

Pressure ridges rose around the hull. The ice twisted. The sound, the men said later, was like a giant creature in pain: groans, shrieks, cracks like gunfire. Endurance, built to be strong, was being bent.

Then the rudder broke. The timbers began to split. Water poured in faster than the pumps could clear it.

On the twenty-seventh of October, Shackleton gave the order the men had been dreading: abandon ship. They carried what they could onto the ice: tents, sledges, food, three lifeboats, and a few personal things. Shackleton insisted on strict limits. Gold watches and coins went on the snow. Hurley kept only about a hundred and twenty of his glass plates and smashed the rest.

For almost a month, they camped next to the dying ship. On the twenty-first of November, the ice finally took it. Endurance tipped, its stern rose into the air, and then it slid beneath the pack.

Shackleton told the men, in effect: the ship is gone. We go home. And the only road home is across the ice.

The nearest help was hundreds of miles away. And nobody in the world knew where they were.

## 4. OCEAN CAMP AND PATIENCE CAMP (7:00-9:00)

Their first plan was simple: drag the lifeboats across the ice toward land. It failed within days. The ice was too broken, the snow too soft, the boats too heavy. They made a few miles in a week, and then Shackleton called it off.

So they camped on the floe, and waited for the drift to carry them north. They called it Ocean Camp, and later, Patience Camp.

Months passed. Food shrank. The dogs, which had been the whole point of the expedition, could not be fed, and one by one they had to be shot. Seal and penguin meat became the staple. The men burned blubber for warmth and cooked in the smoke.

The floe they lived on shrank and cracked. In one night, a crack opened under a tent. A man in his sleeping bag went into the water and was dragged out in seconds.

Slowly, the floe carried them north, out of the deep Weddell Sea. By April 1916, they could see, through the haze, the tips of mountains: land, for the first time in over a year.

But the ice around them was breaking apart. They needed to get off the floe before it dissolved, in the three lifeboats they had hauled for months.

On the ninth of April, they pushed the boats into open water, and the whole crew set off.

## 5. THE BOATS (9:00-10:30)

For seven days, twenty-eight men rowed and sailed three open boats through freezing seas, between icebergs, in temperatures well below freezing.

Spray froze on their clothes. Their sleeping bags were soaked. They were desperately short of drinking water, and some men drank seawater, which only made it worse. Frostbite spread. A few were close to losing their minds.

They landed on Elephant Island, in the South Shetland Islands, around the middle of April. It was a bare, wind-beaten strip of rock and ice. And for the first time in nearly five hundred days, they stood on solid ground.

Some of them laughed. Some knelt. Some simply collapsed.

But Elephant Island was not safe. It was far from any shipping lane. Nobody would look for them there. The nearest whaling station, on South Georgia, was almost eight hundred nautical miles away across the Southern Ocean.

Shackleton looked at the facts and made a decision that sounds insane. If help was not coming, someone had to go and get it.

## 6. THE JAMES CAIRD (10:30-12:45)

The boat they chose was the James Caird, a twenty-two-foot wooden lifeboat. The carpenter, Harry McNish, built it up with timber from the ship's crates and sealed it with canvas, paint, and seal blood.

Shackleton picked five men: Worsley for navigation, Crean, McNish, and two sailors, McCarthy and Vincent.

On the twenty-fourth of April, the twenty-two men left on Elephant Island watched six of their friends push off and disappear. Frank Wild took command of those who stayed, turning two upturned boats into a hut.

What followed was one of the greatest small-boat voyages in history.

The Southern Ocean has no land to stop its waves. Swells of fifty, sixty feet roll around the world there. Worsley had to find a speck of land with a sextant, and he could only take a sight of the sun a handful of times, when the clouds opened and the boat held still for a second.

Ice built up on the hull until the boat was dangerously heavy. Men crawled out to chip it off. Everything was wet. Their feet were swollen, and their clothes were rotting. They took turns sleeping in bags that were soaked with seawater and reindeer hair.

One night, Shackleton saw a line of white on the horizon, thinking it was a break in the clouds. Then he realized it was a wave, and shouted: "For God's sake, hold on! It's got us!" The wave was the largest of the voyage, and the boat was nearly sunk. They bailed all night and survived.

After about two weeks, the Caird reached South Georgia. Almost out of water, they landed on the wrong side of the island, on the uninhabited west coast. Their goal, the whaling station of Stromness, was on the other side, across mountains that no one had ever crossed.

## 7. THE MOUNTAINS AND THE RESCUE (12:45-14:15)

Their boat was badly damaged. Three of the men, too weak to continue, stayed behind. Shackleton, Worsley, and Crean set out to cross the island on foot.

They had no maps and no proper climbing gear. They used a carpenter's adze, a length of rope, and screws from the boat hammered into their boots for grip. They climbed through the night under a full moon, and when fog closed in, they slid down a mountainside on a coil of rope because the alternative was freezing to death.

After thirty-six hours, they heard a noise that cut through the silence: a factory whistle at Stromness, calling workers at seven in the morning. It was the first human sound they had heard in about a year and a half.

When they walked into the station, the manager did not recognise them. They were filthy, bearded, and thin as ghosts. The first man he asked said his name was Shackleton. And the manager is said to have turned away to cry.

The three men on the other coast were rescued first. Then Shackleton set out to save the men on Elephant Island. It took him four attempts. Ice turned back three ships. For months, he begged for help from governments and pleaded for a ship.

Finally, the Chilean Navy lent him a small steamer, the Yelcho. On the thirtieth of August, 1916, in a gap in the ice, it reached Elephant Island.

As the ship came close, Shackleton counted figures on the beach. Then, he is said to have called out: "Are you all well?" And Frank Wild shouted back: "We are all well, boss."

All twenty-two men had survived more than four months alone on that rock.

## 8. AFTERMATH (14:15-15:00)

Not one man of the Endurance's crew was lost. Not to ice, not to cold, not to hunger.

The cost, though, was not zero. On the other side of the continent, the Ross Sea party, the group meant to lay supplies for a crossing that never happened, endured a terrible ordeal of its own, and three of its men died.

The First World War was already raging when the survivors got home, and many of them went straight to the front. Some did not come back.

Shackleton himself never stopped exploring. In 1922 he died of a heart attack in South Georgia, aboard another ship, at the age of forty-seven. He was buried there, close to the mountains he had crossed.

And in March 2022, a hundred and seven years after it sank, the wreck of the Endurance was found, three thousand metres down in the Weddell Sea. It was sitting upright, almost intact, with the ship's name still readable on the stern.

Shackleton never reached the goal he set out for. By any normal measure, the expedition failed completely. But he brought home every man, and that is why we still tell this story.

Because in the end, the question was never whether they could cross a continent. It was whether they could survive together. And they did.
