My mother's coat hangs on the peg behind my hut door, where it has hung since the tide brought it back, a week after.

I held the left cuff to the fibres from the rope. The same grey. The same twist. The cuff was worn through, the way a sleeve wears when a hand has pulled hard on something wet.

Then I read the keeper's margin line. He had written that the bell kept ringing after the road shut. The line was slack at the hut.

A slack line means no hand at the hut.

In the strongroom I held a lamp low across the ledgers. Ink sits on paper. Pencil sinks into it. In the IN column of every page there was a groove. Kell's square strokes. Wenna's round ones. Hands I did not know.

That was the grey smear on my new page each dawn, rubbed out with bread for forty-one years.

That night I sat on the Stone with a dark lantern and watched my own hut.

At the dead of low water, the door opened.

A woman came out in a white nightdress, barefoot, her left hand held a little away from her side. She walked like someone being led by the smell of home. She went to the post, took the short rope in her left hand, and rang the bell.

It rang for a long time. It sounded like what it was, a call to somebody still out in the dark.

Then the lamps came down the hill, eleven of them. They made a ring around her, not touching. Kell took her elbow so gently that she did not stir. Wenna held a lamp to her face and said, "There you are."

Kell stayed. He took the pencil from behind his ear. He opened my book on the desk, found the new page, and wrote in the right column. In one. He wrote it the way a man writes his own name.

He looked up and saw me in the doorway. He held out the pencil.
