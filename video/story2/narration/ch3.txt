The village has an answer for the bell. Visitors see lamps on the causeway some nights, small and slow, in a row.

I do not accept lamps. I accept evidence. I keep an atlas of the causeway, because wet sand holds a print for hours, and a print is a fact. In forty-one years it has recorded every boot, every dog, and one cart. It has recorded no ghost.

Wenna, at the lamp shop, would not turn round from her wicks. I took the second page to Kell, the ferryman. His thumb was grey with graphite, from the pencil he keeps behind his ear for the nets. He looked at the table. He did not look at the page. "Nobody came home that night, Maren," he said. "That is why you keep the book."

So the bell rang, the road was empty, and the ledger was one short. Three facts that cannot all be true. I decided to remove the one fact I could control. Myself.

At seven I fell asleep once, with the rope in my hand. I would not sleep again. I took a length of chain from the harbour store and set my desk where the line comes through the wall. One end went round the desk leg. The other went round my left ankle.

A chain is not for keeping something out. It is for staying put when the body would rather go.

At the dead of low water the bell rings. The lamps come out on the road. The road is empty.

I turned the key and hung it on a cord round my neck. I began to count.
