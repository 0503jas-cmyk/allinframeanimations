At the next low water I went to the post.
I stood on its sea side, my back to the water, and took the short rope in my left hand.
I rang the bell as the tide began to climb.
The sea came over the stone and found my ankles.
It was colder than I had allowed for.
It climbed my shins, and then my knees, and the rope grew heavy in my fist.
My palm lay in the worn groove.
Beside it, under my little finger, was the second groove, deeper, cut by a sleeve.
I looked toward the village lamps, the way a small child would have gone, barefoot, asleep, with the bell going behind her.
It is a long road for small legs.
The child was me.
Kell was waiting at the dry end, nineteen, running the wall with a lantern.
The bell rang until the water reached the clapper.
A drowned bell speaks once more.
He heard it speak.
I was across.
She was not.
The cold opened the door the years had locked.
She came back across in the last light, with medicine in her coat.
The hut was empty.
She followed small bare prints to the Stone and found me standing in the water.
She took my hand and walked me to the post, and turned me to face the lamps.
She took the short rope.
The sea was at her knees.
She rang, and I walked.
When I reached the dry stones she said it again, as she had at the door.
"Don't sleep, Maren. Count for me."
I counted my way home.
I wrote the figure on the next page, since a clerk does not write beneath the closing rule.
I left a space for the name and went to bed.
Kell wrote the figure in pencil on the first clean page, and Wenna on the next, one of them every night for forty-one years.
A child who learns she walks into the sea never sleeps again, so they chose the pencil over the telling, even on the chained night.
I had rubbed out every one.
I opened the old ledger to the second page.
In the blank, in a hand that did not shake, I wrote my own name.
Maren Voss.
Then I turned back to the straight stroke of a proud child in the OUT column and inked a name over it.
The first name that column has ever held.
Elin Voss.
