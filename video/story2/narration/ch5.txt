The Drowning Stone is ruled like a ledger.

I had never come close enough to see it. It stands beyond the post, where the road dips, and at low water it shows its whole back, black and wet. Up close it is cut with short strokes, packed edge to edge. Forty-one rows, one for each year, and in each row a stroke for every night.

I walked to the end of the newest row. Stroke after stroke after stroke, a thumb's width apart, one place for every night. The last stroke was two nights old. The place for last night was smooth.

I knew which night. I walked back to the post.

I had pulled that line from the hut ten thousand times. I had never stood where the short rope hangs and looked at it from the sea. It was polished flat, as if a thumb had been drawn along it for decades. On one side only. The sea side.

A rope pulled from the hut wears all round. This one had been pulled by someone standing out on the road with her back to the water, always with the same hand. I laid my palm in the groove. My fingers closed over the shine as though it had been cut for them.

I am left-handed.

For forty-one years I had listened for a ghost on the road. The one who walks the causeway every night, and is never written down, is the clerk. The OUT that was never counted was me.

The tide clock turned. I did not hear the bell.
