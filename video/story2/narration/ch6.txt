I wanted the feet.

That evening I scattered flour across the hut floor, from the bed to the door. I did not chain my ankle. I lay down in my clothes with my eyes open as long as I could keep them.

In the morning the flour had a path in it.

Bare feet, the second toe longer than the first. From the bed to the door. The door was latched from outside.

I followed the prints along the crown of the road on my knees. Every fifth step they leaned a little to the left, as if someone were walking with a hand held out. They reached the post and stopped, facing the village.

Then more prints. Boots, a ring of them, around mine. They had not trodden on one print of mine.

I sat on the wet stone and let it be true. She walks barefoot every night, and the flood tide takes her prints before I wake.

The small bare prints in the atlas. They were mine.

But a woman who finds her child on a closing road carries her home and shuts the door. Her heels did not go home. I could not make that sentence add up, so I put it aside.

I went back to the rope, because a rope remembers. Under the bright groove of my own left hand there was another. Older. Deeper. Higher up the rope, at the reach of a taller woman. And worked into the strands, grey wool.

I knew only one coat that colour.
