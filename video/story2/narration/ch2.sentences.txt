The first page is plain.
The date is in my mother's hand, because she dated the week ahead every Sunday.
Below it is one stroke in the OUT column, in mine.
That stroke is her.
She crossed to the mainland at dusk for the morning medicine, because I had a fever.
At the door she turned and said, "Don't sleep, Maren. Count for me."
She smelled of wet wool and the peppermints she kept for the bell watch.
I counted her out, and I was proud of the stroke, because it was straight.
The IN column beside it has been empty for forty-one years.
The second page I never knew existed.
It carries the date of the following night.
In the right-hand column, in brown ink, in a hand that is mine at seven, all loops and pressure, there is an entry.
In one.
Beside it, where a name belongs, there is a space.
I do not remember writing it.
I remember the lamp, the fever, the rope in my fist.
After that, nothing.
I reasoned as a clerk reasons.
Somebody came home that night.
I wrote the figure and could not write the name.
A name I owe the book.
I allow no pencil in my book.
Yet every morning there is a grey smear on the new page, in the right column, and every morning I rub it out with a crust of bread.
Tonight I sat up with the ledger and the lamp.
I woke in my chair with the bell still dying in the dark.
My hem was stiff with salt.
The Ghost rings at the dead of low water, when the moon stands over the road.
I went to the window and looked at the road.
Nobody was on it.
